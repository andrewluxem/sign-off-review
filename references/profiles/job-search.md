# Profile: job-search

**Use for:** resume, cover letter, LinkedIn About, interview stories, reviewed together as one application.
**Signals:** roles with dates, achievement metrics, a target role or job description.
**Target role:** pass the job description. Without one, the Decision seat names the role it assumed, and the review's Question line marks it as an assumption.

## Seat 1: Decision (hiring manager)

- **1.1** Read only the top third of page 1 (name line, headline, summary, first role). Can you say what role this person is for, in the job description's own terms? If you have to scroll to find out, that is a finding.
- **1.2** The two or three requirements the JD weights most each have visible proof near the top of the resume and in the cover letter's first paragraph.
- **1.3** Scope (team size, budget, revenue, audience) signals the level the JD asks for, not a level below or above.
- **1.4** The cover letter's opening names this company and this role and says why this candidate fits. It could not be sent unchanged to another employer.

## Seat 2: Logic

- **2.1** You can state the candidate's positioning in one sentence from the documents alone, and every document (resume, cover letter, About, stories) supports that same sentence.
- **2.2** Each resume bullet supports the positioning. Bullets that tell a different story are named for cutting or moving lower.
- **2.3** Within each role, the bullet most relevant to the target role comes first, and the summary and titles point the same way.
- **2.4** Every claim in the cover letter or About is backed by the resume. Nothing in the letter is new or bigger.
- **2.5** Each interview story has a situation, the candidate's own action and a result, and the result ties back to the positioning.

## Seat 3: Evidence

- **3.1** Every metric states its scope (what was measured, over which program, channel or audience), a baseline, a timeframe, and the candidate's share (owned it, led a team of N, contributed).
- **3.2** Percentages and ratios have a base: "up 30 percent" says from what, and over what period.
- **3.3** Superlatives (first, only, largest, record) name what they are compared against, or they go.
- **3.4** Team results are not written as individual results. "I" and "we" match the role described.
- **3.5** Named clients, employers and figures are safe to disclose: nothing confidential, nothing a former employer would dispute.

## Seat 4: Skeptic (recruiter)

- **4.1** Every role has start and end dates as month and year in one format, and the same dates appear in every document. Overlapping roles are explained.
- **4.2** Gaps of six months or more, including the time since the last role ended, are visible and explained in a line, not hidden by year-only dates.
- **4.3** Tenures under a year carry a reason (contract, acquisition, layoff, relocation) or are grouped.
- **4.4** Titles match across documents and match what a reference check would confirm.
- **4.5** The JD's must-have terms (skills, platforms, methods) appear word for word wherever they are true. List the missing ones, and never suggest adding one the documents do not support.

## Seat 5: Outsider (non-specialist)

- **5.1** A reader outside CRM and lifecycle marketing can say what business result each top achievement produced (revenue, cost, retention, customers) without decoding jargon.
- **5.2** Acronyms, tool names and internal program names are spelled out, or paired with what they did, on first use.
- **5.3** Each top bullet states its business effect itself instead of leaving the reader to infer it.
- **5.4** The documents make sense in the order a stranger reads them: no project, team or term is referenced before it is introduced.

## Tie-out scope

Every metric, date range, title and employer name across all documents passed in. A metric in the resume that differs from the cover letter is a Blocker, and so is a start or end date that differs between documents.

## Outside view

Here the outside reference is the job description. Name each must-have requirement that has no evidence in the documents. Do not invent applicant-pool, salary or market data.

## Premortem prompts

Internal (on the candidate's side):
- Positioning too broad: the candidate reads as a fit for everything and a standout for nothing.
- A number the candidate cannot defend in an interview, or that a reference would dispute.
- The documents tell the career the candidate had, not the role they want next.
- Seniority signals sit one level off the role.

External:
- An internal candidate or a referral takes the slot.
- An applicant tracking system filters on a missing must-have term.
- The role's scope changes after posting.

## False alarms (job-search)

- A bullet without a metric where no honest number exists. A concrete scope or outcome is enough.
- A gap already explained in one line.
- Resume length, when the top third does its job.
- No objective statement.
- A short cover letter.
- A missing keyword the candidate cannot truthfully claim. Never ask for it to be added.
