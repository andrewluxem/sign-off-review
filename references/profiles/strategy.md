# Profile: strategy

**Use for:** memos, plans and decks that ask a reader for a decision.
**Signals:** a decision requested, options, costs or savings, a recommendation.

## Seat 1: Decision (decision maker)

- **1.1** The first page, first slide or first paragraph answers the question in one sentence. A reader who stops there knows what is recommended.
- **1.2** The recommendation names who acts, what they do, by when, and with what budget or resources.
- **1.3** The reader's own decision is explicit: approve, choose between named options, or note.
- **1.4** Conditions the decision depends on (an approval, a deadline, a prerequisite) appear where the decision is asked for.

## Seat 2: Logic

- **2.1** Read only the headings or slide titles, in order. They tell the argument on their own.
- **2.2** The supporting points are complete for the recommendation and do not overlap: no reason the conclusion needs is missing, and no two sections make the same point.
- **2.3** The recommendation follows from the analysis. Nothing appears for the first time in the conclusion.
- **2.4** Credible alternatives, including doing nothing, are named and ruled out with a reason.

## Seat 3: Evidence

- **3.1** Every number has a source, a date and a unit.
- **3.2** Each key claim rests on two independent kinds of evidence (for example internal data and an external reference), or the draft says it rests on one.
- **3.3** Assumptions are marked as assumptions, separate from facts.
- **3.4** Evidence that cuts against the recommendation is shown and answered, not left out.

## Seat 4: Skeptic (CFO)

- **4.1** Every figure that appears more than once has the same value everywhere: summary, body, tables, appendix.
- **4.2** Totals equal the sum of their parts and rates follow from their inputs. Show the arithmetic for any that fail.
- **4.3** Each main driver (saving, growth, cost, timing) is compared with an outside reference: a past result, a base rate or a cited benchmark.
- **4.4** A downside case exists: what happens if the main driver comes in worse, and what would trigger a reversal.
- **4.5** No model red flags: growth that bends sharply upward without a cause, costs that stay flat while volume grows, missing one-time costs, a payback quoted without its inputs.

## Seat 5: Outsider (non-specialist)

- **5.1** A smart reader from another function can follow the draft without a glossary. Terms and acronyms are explained on first use.
- **5.2** The main line is short, and the detail sits later or in an appendix. The reader does not wade through method to reach the answer.
- **5.3** Every table and chart says what it shows in its title, with units on each column or axis.
- **5.4** The draft stands without the meeting it was written for: no "as discussed" context the reader lacks.

## Tie-out scope

Every figure in the draft, including tables, appendices, footnotes and speaker notes. Dates, deadlines and headcounts count as figures.

## Outside view

Name the comparison each driver needs: a past project, a base rate, a peer. Flag a benchmark with no source. Do not supply one.

## Premortem prompts

Internal (on the author's or client's side):
- No named owner, so the plan stalls after approval.
- The team that must deliver is already at capacity.
- Incentives point elsewhere: the people who must act lose budget, headcount or status.
- The plan depends on a structure, role or system that does not exist yet.
- The authors' blind spot: the analysis found what they hoped to find.

External:
- A key assumption (price, demand, timing) moves.
- A dependency (supplier, regulator, partner) slips.
- A competitor or market responds.

## False alarms (strategy)

- No "executive summary" heading when the first paragraph does that job.
- One recommended option plus a reason for ruling out each alternative. Do not ask for a scored options matrix.
- Summary figures that match the detail after rounding.
- No sensitivity table when the downside case is already stated.
