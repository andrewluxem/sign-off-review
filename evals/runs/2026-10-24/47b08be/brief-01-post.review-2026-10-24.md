# Sign-off review: brief-01-post.md, profile brief-post, stage 3
**Verdict:** Return
**In one line:** The brief tells every retention marketer to move every campaign to 6 a.m. on the strength of one retailer's uncontrolled first month of opens, so as written it would mislead the readers it addresses.
**Question:** What should a retention marketer change on Monday, and why? (given)
**Answer the draft gives:** "Move every campaign to 6 a.m. in each subscriber's local time", because "the first email in the inbox wins", shown by one furniture retailer whose opens "rose 18 percent in the first month" after a move from 10 a.m.
**Assumptions:** Profile brief-post detected from the content (a weekly brief for retention marketers with a hook, a thesis and a Monday takeaway) and not confirmed with the author. Stage 3 (final) by default, not confirmed. "Last year" is read against the 2026-10-24 review date as 2025. The draft names no source for any external figure, so the outside view had nothing to fetch and check, and no source was searched for on the author's behalf. All five seats ran as independent subagents.

## Missing outside view
- "73 percent of marketers say send time is the biggest lever on open rate" (line 7): no source named, so nothing could be fetched or checked this session. See finding 3.
- "Studies show that subscribers decide what to open in the first minutes of their day" (line 7): no study named; unverified. See finding 3.
- "Most email programs leave opens on the table" (line 5) and "Your subscribers check email before work" (line 11): no base rate for when programs send or when subscribers check. See findings 3 and 5.
- The 18 percent rise in opens (line 9): a first-person result with no control, no named prior period and no other segment to compare with. See finding 2.
- 6 a.m. as the right hour: compared only with 10 a.m., never with other hours. See finding 6.

## Premortem
1. A retention marketer who has run their own send-time tests replies that their lift came at a different hour or faded after a month, and the author cannot defend "every campaign" because the only first-hand support is one retailer's first month of opens. (internal)
2. A reader asks where the 73 percent figure and "the studies" come from, no source can be produced, and the list discounts the whole brief, including the real retailer result.
3. Readers who make the Monday change see opens move while orders stay flat, because the brief measured only opens, and they stop acting on its recommendations.

## What is good and must survive
- The separate, bolded "What to do on Monday" line at the close: one concrete action that answers the question directly.
- The first-person furniture retailer case with a specific before and after (weekly promotion, 10 a.m. to 6 a.m., first month, the author's advisory role). It is the draft's only checkable evidence; expand it, do not replace it.
- The tagline naming the reader, "a weekly brief for retention marketers".
- The precise timing rule, "6 a.m. in each subscriber's local time", which makes the action testable.
- The short length and plain vocabulary, with no unexplained trade terms.

## What fixed looks like
The Monday action is a scoped test: scheduled promotional sends move to an earlier hour against a holdout kept at the current hour, with a named retention metric, a test length, and stated exclusions (triggered sends, audiences who do not check before work, lists without time-zone data). The retailer result appears as one case with its comparison period or holdout, the months involved, what else changed, a defined open-rate change with its baseline, and a downstream result such as orders or repeat purchases. The reason for 6 a.m. is one stated mechanism that meets the inbox-order objection. Every external claim either carries a fetched source with its population, date and measure or is cut, and the headline claims no more than the comparison the body makes.
